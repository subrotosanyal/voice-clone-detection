Build status — what's actually running today

Everything below §00 is the original blueprint, unchanged in its reasoning. This section is the reality check on top of it: what got built, what still hasn't, and roughly when. Read this section first if all you have time for is one.

The short version

The pluggable core — acoustic, prosodic, and third-signal detection (now including real Whisper transcription feeding automatic urgency/financial-language detection), fused and explained transparently, with a working dashboard, persistent history, voiceprint enrollment, and speaker diarization — is built, running in Docker, and covered by 95 passing automated tests. §04, the Indian-language differentiator, has started, and its scope has been deliberately narrowed to English and Hindi only, for now — Marathi and Malvi are no longer required (see the scope note in §04). Real, license-verified genuine Hindi speech and real XTTS-v2 Hindi spoof clones have been scored and used to fine-tune AASIST, giving a first real number — 10% EER for Hindi, unchanged before/after fine-tuning, an honest finding rather than an improvement. Still missing: an independently-measured English baseline EER against a public benchmark, and a second held-out Hindi synthesis system (needed to prove generalisation, not just recalibration). That gap is still the one most likely to decide the round.

01

What this is

From the brief — read this first

Voice deepfakes are a fashionable topic; the pool of competing teams will not stay small. The Indian-language evaluation in §04 is the one part of this build a competing team is unlikely to have — that work starts in week one, not week four.

One sentence the whole team should be able to repeat back: software that listens to a live call and works out, while the call is still happening, whether the voice on the other end came from a human throat or a machine — and shows a risk score before anyone approves anything.

The attack. Modern speech synthesis clones a voice convincingly from a few seconds of recorded audio — a talk, a voice note, a TV clip. An attacker calls a bank employee or a junior official sounding exactly like the CFO or the Secretary, applies time pressure, and extracts a transfer or a credential. Caller ID is trivially spoofed, recognising the voice is precisely what the attack defeats, and a call-back only works if the target thinks to do it under pressure.

Why detection is possible. A neural vocoder reconstructs a waveform rather than recording one, and the reconstruction leaves traces — phase relationships that don't occur naturally, spectral envelopes that are too smooth, timing that's subtly too regular. A human vocal tract adds breath, micro-tremor, jitter and shimmer that synthesis approximates rather than reproduces.

The framing that matters to a judge

This system is not asking who is speaking — that's speaker verification. It's asking how the signal was made. Say that distinction out loud: it's the reason this stops the fraud that voice-recognition alone cannot.

Build this, skip that

The brief lists REST/gRPC APIs, SDKs, telecom integration, compliance modules and multi-channel alerting. Building all of it produces a slide deck, not a system.

Build

  • Live spoof detection on a streaming call
  • A continuously updating risk score
  • One operator dashboard
  • One mock banking approval that blocks on high risk
  • Indian-language evaluation set and results

Skip

  • SDKs for third-party platforms
  • Telecom operator infrastructure integration
  • Multi-channel alerting (SMS, email, in-app)
  • A configurable enterprise workflow engine
  • Full compliance and retention tooling
Say it on stage

"We built depth on detection rather than breadth on integration." Framed that way, the omission reads as judgement, not as an unfinished project.

A note on named tools — updated with what actually shipped

The candidates in the original table were starting points to verify, not commitments — and verifying them is exactly what happened: AASIST (MIT), Parselmouth (GPLv3), and SpeechBrain's ECAPA-TDNN (Apache-2.0) were each checked for licence terms and confirmed installable with no authentication/gating before being wired in (pyannote.audio was considered for diarization and rejected — its pretrained pipelines are all gated on HuggingFace, which would break a "docker compose up, no account needed" build). FastRTC and a separate SSL embedding stage were evaluated conceptually and dropped once the simpler Web-Audio+WebSocket and AASIST's-own-front-end approaches proved sufficient for this scope.

03

The risk score

The acoustic model gives one number. What the operator sees should be more than that number, and it should be legible: three components, each with its own bar, combined by a visible formula — not a second model.

LayerContributesDifficulty
AcousticProbability the waveform was synthesised — the core signalThe main build
ProsodicRhythm, pitch contour, pause distribution, breath placement — synthesis is often too evenModerate, worth adding if time allows
Third signalConfig-selectable — see belowDepends on which mode

Pluggable third signal — chosen at runtime, not hardcoded

The brief's contextual layer (cheap, no enrollment) and a speaker-consistency check (stronger, more infrastructure) solve a similar problem differently. Rather than pick one and lose the other, wire the fusion engine to read which one is active from config — swap it per environment, per demo, or per how much of the team's time is left.

Contextual signal

built THIRD_SIGNAL_MODE = "contextual"

Rule-based: is the number known? First contact? Unusual hour? Is the request financial? Does urgency language appear in the transcript? The transcript itself is now real — Whisper (MIT) transcribes the call automatically, and a transparent English/Hindi/Marathi keyword list detects urgency/financial-request language, merged with anything supplied by hand rather than replacing it.

Pros
  • No ML or enrollment infra — a rules engine, buildable in hours
  • Zero privacy/consent burden — no voiceprint ever stored
  • Legible to judges — each rule is plain English on the bar
  • Works from the first call — no cold-start
Cons
  • Weak alone — an attacker who knows the target's number and calls in business hours evades it entirely
  • Static rules, easy to game once known
  • Estimates plausibility of context, not identity

Speaker consistency

built THIRD_SIGNAL_MODE = "consistency"

Embedding similarity between the live stream and a voiceprint enrolled ahead of time for the claimed identity — built on SpeechBrain's ECAPA-TDNN (Apache-2.0), with a working enrollment flow (enroll / list / delete). The cosine-similarity thresholds are an honest placeholder pending calibration on labeled data, not yet a validated cutoff.

Pros
  • Checks identity directly — harder to defeat even for an attacker who knows the system exists
  • Genuinely orthogonal to acoustic: "is this synthetic" vs. "does this match who they claim to be"
  • Answers the sharper judge question: "what if the fraudster avoids the contextual heuristics?"
Cons
  • Needs an enrollment flow, consent record, embedding store, TTL — real extra build days
  • Cold-start: no signal for anyone not yet enrolled
  • Adds a biometric-adjacent privacy surface a lean build may not want to defend under questioning
Config & fallback logic as-built

Built exactly as planned, down to the weights and the fallback order. Default mode is auto: prefer consistency when a voiceprint is on file for claimed_identity, fall back to contextual otherwise, and if neither produces a score fusion renormalises the remaining weights rather than silently scoring a zero — this abstain-aware behaviour is asserted by an automated test, not just described. The real config lives in config/risk_formula.yaml, loaded and swappable at runtime with no code change:

# config/risk_formula.yaml (as actually shipped)
formula_version: "2026.09.4"
detectors:
  - name: acoustic
    class: app.adapters.detectors.acoustic_aasist:AasistAcousticDetector
    weight: 0.6
  - name: prosodic
    class: app.adapters.detectors.prosody_parselmouth:ParselmouthProsodyDetector
    weight: 0.2
  - name: third_signal
    class: app.adapters.detectors.third_signal_router:ThirdSignalRouter
    weight: 0.2
    params:
      mode: auto   # contextual | consistency | auto
      contextual_class: app.adapters.detectors.contextual_rules:ContextualRulesDetector
      consistency_class: app.adapters.detectors.voiceprint_consistency:VoiceprintConsistencyDetector

fusion:
  class: app.adapters.fusion.weighted_sum:WeightedSumFusion
  smoothing_alpha: 0.35   # EMA smoothing across a session's windows

risk = 0.6 × acoustic + 0.2 × prosodic + 0.2 × third — bands

Low
0–34
Consistent with natural speech. Nothing — silence is the correct behaviour.
Elevated
35–69
Some synthesis indicators present. A quiet on-screen advisory, no interruption.
High
70–100
Strong synthesis indicators. Block the pending action; require call-back or a second approver.
Design the false-positive path deliberately

A system that cries wolf on genuine callers gets switched off in week one of real deployment, and a sharp judge will ask about exactly this. The high band never blocks the call itself, only the sensitive action, and there's always a documented path to proceed — call back on a number already on file. Tune thresholds so genuine speech almost never reaches High, and say out loud what that costs in missed detections.

04

The differentiator: Indian languages

started
Status — in progress, real first number in hand

Started, using existing licensed public corpora instead of recruiting classmates first — see eval/indian_language/README.md for the full pipeline. Real, license-verified (CC BY 4.0) genuine Hindi speech plus real XTTS-v2-cloned Hindi speech has been scored through the exact same AASIST model §02 wired in, then AASIST's final layer was actually fine-tuned on it: 10% Equal Error Rate for Hindi, both before and after fine-tuning — fine-tuning shifted the decision threshold (catching every genuine Hindi speaker but missing more clones, 100%→80% spoof accuracy) without improving actual separability, an honest finding given only 50 total examples. Still remaining: an independently-measured English baseline EER against a public benchmark, and — the one that would make "after fine-tuning" mean something — a second, genuinely held-out Hindi synthesis system.

Scope decision — English and Hindi only, for now

The brief's original ask (below) names Hindi, Marathi and Malvi. The team has deliberately narrowed active scope to English (the existing AASIST baseline) and Hindi — Marathi and Malvi are no longer required deliverables. Reasoning: Malvi has no digital resources of any kind and would need a native-speaker volunteer to record from scratch, and no ungated open TTS/cloning system covering Marathi was found, so neither language could reach a real spoof-vs-bonafide measurement in the time available. (A genuine-speech-only check for Marathi was already run as a byproduct before this decision and is kept in eval/indian_language/README.md for reference — it's not part of the tracked scope going forward.) This keeps the two numbers that actually decide the round — English baseline, Hindi before/after fine-tuning — achievable and honest, rather than three partial ones.

From the brief — the single line that decides the project

Public spoof-detection research is overwhelmingly English. Models trained on English corpora degrade markedly on other languages and unfamiliar accents. AICTE explicitly asks for Indian languages and regional accents — almost no team relying on off-the-shelf benchmarks will have measured this gap.

Everything in §01–03 is competent engineering a good team elsewhere will also produce, and it's now genuinely built (see §00). This section is the one most teams skip — this team has now started it, with a real first measurement instead of a plan.

  1. Recruit ~20 classmates speaking Hindi, Marathi and Malvi. Written consent — a one-page form naming exactly what the recordings are used for and how long they're kept.
  2. Record each person reading the same 8–10 sentences, on a phone, in an ordinary room. Real conditions matter more than studio quality.
  3. Generate cloned versions of those same voices using at least two different open-source synthesis systems.
  4. Hold one synthesis system back entirely — never train on it. It's the honest test.
  5. Measure the English-trained model on this set, fine-tune on the Indian-language data, and measure again.
The two numbers that win the round

Accuracy on English benchmarks, and accuracy on Indian languages before any adaptation. The gap will be large — that's the whole argument. Then show it closing after fine-tuning. A genuine, honest, measured contribution from six undergraduates in five weeks, and exactly what the sponsoring body asked for.

Consent and ethics — non-negotiable, set on day one

  • Only voices of consenting team members and volunteers. Never a real official, executive, or public figure, however dramatic the demo would be.
  • Written consent for every recorded voice, kept on file, with a stated retention period.
  • Never publish the cloned audio anywhere. It stays on team machines and is deleted after the event.
"Every voice in this demonstration belongs to someone in this room, with their written consent."

06

Risks, limitations & scale

Known risks the brief already names, then a longer honest look at what this design can't currently do — technically, ethically, and at scale. Naming these is not hedging; a system that claims none of this reads as less credible to a technical judge, not more.

In plain terms

No detector like this is perfect, forever, or ready for the real world on day one — and that's normal, not a flaw specific to this project. This section is the honest version of "what could still go wrong": some of it is technical (the system can be fooled or confused in specific situations), some of it is about fairness and privacy (the system needs to be used carefully and not blindly trusted), and some of it is about size (a five-week student build is not the same thing as a system a bank could actually switch on tomorrow). Knowing exactly where these gaps are is what makes this a serious piece of engineering rather than a demo trick.

RiskMitigationWhat we say
Generalisation — the known hard problemHold back one synthesis system entirely; report against it. Testing only on the generator you trained on produces fiction."Here is our accuracy on a synthesis system we never trained on."
LatencyStreaming from week three. Overlapping windows, rolling smoothing, sub-second updates.Show it moving mid-sentence rather than describing it.
False positivesTune so genuine speech rarely reaches High; never block the call, only the sensitive action.Name the trade-off explicitly and give the missed-detection cost.
Scope creepBuild only the five items in §01's Build column."Depth on detection over breadth on integration" — a choice, not an omission.

Technical limitations

Beyond any single mitigatable risk, these are structural limits of this class of system — worth understanding, not worth pretending don't exist. Each starts with the plain-language version, then the technical detail.

LimitationWhy it matters
Codec & compression sensitivityIn plain terms: squashing a call through a phone network can wipe out the very clues the detector relies on. Real phone audio passes through lossy codecs (AMR-NB/EVS on telecom, Opus on VoIP) that strip exactly the high-frequency and phase artifacts the acoustic countermeasure relies on. A model that scores well on clean ASVspoof-style audio can degrade sharply once it's actually carried over a call — this is precisely why ASVspoof5 built codec robustness into its baseline. Evaluate on codec-compressed copies of the eval set before trusting the accuracy number.
Short-window signal starvationIn plain terms: a short, quiet, or noisy snippet may not give the system enough to work with. A 2-second window is short. Quiet speech, cross-talk, or a noisy line can leave the acoustic model with too little signal to be confident — and that uncertainty needs to be visible on the dashboard as a confidence value, not hidden inside a single score that looks equally sure either way.
Adversarial arms raceIn plain terms: because the method is built from public research, someone determined enough could eventually study the same papers and try to beat it. Detection built partly from published, open research (AASIST, RawGAT-ST) can in principle be tuned against by an attacker with the same papers. This is not a solvable-once problem — the honest framing is "raises the cost of the attack," not "closes it."
Voiceprint drift & cold startIn plain terms: a person's voice changes over time, and anyone who never "signed up" in advance gets no benefit from this particular check. Consistency mode only: illness, aging, emotional state, or a bad phone mic can shift a voice enough to look like a mismatch against an old enrollment, and anyone never enrolled gets no signal from this layer at all — exactly why §03 makes it config-selectable with a contextual fallback rather than failing silently.
Fusion is only as strong as its weakest live signalIn plain terms: if one of the three checks can't run, the score is quietly based on the other two — and the screen has to say so, not hide it. If the third signal abstains — no enrollment on file, no usable call metadata — fusion is quietly running on two components, not three. A score that doesn't say so is misleading; the per-component bars in §03 exist specifically so a missing signal is visible rather than absorbed into one number.

Ethical considerations

§04 covers consent for recording voices used in the build. This is broader — the ethical exposure of the system itself, once it exists.

  • Automation bias. In plain terms: people tend to trust a number just because it's a number, even when it's wrong. An operator who sees a score tends to trust the score. A "Low" reading on a genuinely dangerous call is still a real failure mode — the interface must read as an advisory, never as a verdict, and the operator keeps the decision either way.
  • Differential accuracy is a fairness problem, not just an engineering one. In plain terms: if the system is trained mostly on some accents or languages, it may work worse for exactly the speakers it's meant to protect. A near-certainty on a five-week build (by §04's own admission) means the false-negative rate is higher for underrepresented voices — the group the Indian-language differentiator is meant to protect could be the least protected in practice, until the fine-tuning claimed in §04 is verified end to end, not just measured in aggregate.
  • Dual-use risk. In plain terms: explaining exactly how we catch fake voices could also teach someone how to avoid getting caught. The same acoustic and prosodic signals that reveal synthetic speech are a reasonable guide to what a cloning system would need to fix to evade detection. Publishing results responsibly means sharing the honest accuracy numbers without also publishing a ready-made "how to beat this" recipe.
  • Privacy of the embedding, not just the audio. In plain terms: even a mathematical fingerprint of someone's voice is still personal information about them. "We don't store raw audio" is true and necessary, but a voiceprint embedding is still biometric data — it needs the same seriousness the brief already gives audio retention, including a real deletion path tied to consent withdrawal, not just a TTL number in a design doc.
  • Reputational harm of a false positive. In plain terms: quietly flagging someone as a possible fake can unfairly embarrass a real, innocent caller if it happens by mistake. Flagging a genuine caller as a probable clone — even privately, to one operator — is not a neutral event for that person if it recurs or leaks. The operator-facing language should match §03's framing: an advisory prompting a call-back, never an accusation.

Scaling: prototype vs. production

Everything below is out of scope by design (§01's Skip column) — but a judge asking "what happens next" deserves an honest answer, not a claim that the prototype already handles it.

ConcernWhere the prototype stopsWhat production actually needs
ConcurrencyOne call at a time, single-process inferenceIn plain terms: a real call centre handles thousands of calls at once, not one. Batched/quantised inference and autoscaled workers, not one FastAPI process.

ComplianceExplicitly out of scope (§01)In plain terms: a real bank or government deployment has legal obligations this prototype was deliberately built to skip for now. Data-protection law, telecom regulation, and audit-trail requirements.
Say it out loud

"This prototype proves the detection mechanism works. It does not yet prove it scales, and we can tell you exactly what would have to be true for it to." That sentence, said before a judge asks, reads as engineering judgement — the same move §01 already makes about integration breadth, applied here to production readiness.

The one-sentence success bar

A sceptical judge watches a genuine and a cloned clip diverge live, asks what happens with background noise and gets a straight adversarial-test answer, then asks what happens to voice data and gets a straight privacy answer. Everything above exists to make that moment survive contact with someone who pushes back.

Glossary

Countermeasure (CM): the research term for a spoof-detection model — what the acoustic detector in §02 wraps.

EER (Equal Error Rate): the point where false-accept and false-reject rates are equal; lower is better.

Embedding: a fixed-length vector representing a voice, used for similarity comparison instead of storing raw audio — only relevant in consistency mode.

Held-out generator: a voice-cloning tool deliberately excluded from training, used only to measure honest generalisation.
